EXERCISE - PHILOSOPHY: TO BUY OR NOT BUY THE KAYAK

I had taken a long break from mountain biking and was looking for a way back in. I was 60% sure I wanted to, but not completely convinced. Inspired by a recent Richard Branson story in which he initially leased his planes before buying them, I found the cheapest brand new bike I could. $100 and it was as terrible as $100 would allow. I loved it for the first few months and rode the wheels off it—literally until the chain broke, wheels bent, brakes turned to dust and the handlebars began to untension. I was almost there with wanting a bike, but not quite. Fortunately an old client of mine offered up his mountain bike to borrow. No strings attached, he wasn’t using it (“in the garage collecting dust, commuting to a new job etc”) and wanted to let me try a “proper” bike. And proper it was. Proper suspension, disc brakes (when they were rare and expensive) and proper mountain bike tires that could chew through mud. That bike was a blast. I was convinced and three months later, handed my old client's bike back (cleaned and with tremendous thanks) and purchased my own—the exact same model as his.

It took me 6 months to figure out that mountain biking was my thing, and it still is during the summer seasons. I’m surrounded by hills and trails and a decent mountain bike makes these experiences much more enjoyable.

One thing I still haven't, and am unsure if I ever will purchase, is a kayak.

When spring arrives I start seeing more boats on the water. Fishing, rowing, jet skis and kayaks. I start thinking about how cool it would be to have my own. I could paddle wherever I want, whenever. Fish, paddle down rapids, go camping on empty islands etc. Ultimately, I end up renting one for a few hours a few times from a shop by the wharf. This is not because I don’t enjoy it, or couldn’t find a cheap one online to bang around in. It’s down to two main reasons. Owning a kayak means storing a kayak, cleaning a kayak, having a life jacket, paddle, car to transport it and more time on either end getting ready. And living 30 minutes from the ocean means more time, more effort and more admin. I can turn up to the store, and be on the water in 10 minutes for two hours, paddle up to shore and simply leave and get on with my day. Yes, I can’t paddle to that secret island in the middle of the harbour, or take it down some rapids. Perhaps one day.

Owning all the gear for an activity can be helpful, and is a catalyst for some to get into a hobby. For others, dipping our toes, entering it cheaply without buyer's remorse or a full garage of “things” gives us the ability to try more things and exit if it’s not for us.

My first drum kit was a set of ice cream containers, chopsticks and Chupa Chups tins.

My second drum kit was a $300 piece of trash that never seemed to stay in tune or in place.

My third drum kit was a beautiful Yamaha Stage Custom Advantage that I owned for 15 years.

My friend's first drum kit was a kit that was as good as my third. He played it as hard as I did for a while then stopped. I’m not sure he plays anymore.

You may need your own things, brand new even, to start a hobby. It might be a signal to yourself that “this is the time to take it seriously”.

Or a signal to others that you’re invested. Hopefully it’s not, though—this is your journey.

You might need a pair of second-hand hiking boots, your parents' backpack and a tent with metal poles and a leaky roof to see if the great outdoors is for you.

Whatever the case, don’t waste your money if you can save it for when you really know the hobby is for you.
